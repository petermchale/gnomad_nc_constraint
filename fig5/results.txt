Gnocchi's cutoff does not mean the same thing across the genome, and retraining makes it do so. A z-score promises that a given value denotes the same departure from neutral expectation wherever it is measured, and that promise is what licenses attaching a single genome-wide cutoff to it. We tested it directly, using no truth set of any kind, by asking where a fixed cutoff falls within the score distribution of each GC bin (Fig. 5F). Chen et al.'s own cutoff of Gnocchi >= 4, at which they define constrained noncoding regions, sits at the 99.850th percentile of the most AT-rich windows drawn and the 58.3rd percentile of the most GC-rich, calling 0.150% of windows in the first and 41.7% in the last -- a 277-fold swing in how much sequence a single numeral selects. Genome-wide that same cutoff sits at the 99.34th percentile, and because the bins' calling rates average to that value and percentile is an affine function of calling rate, a score meeting its own promise would trace a horizontal line there and no other. Published Gnocchi crosses that line only at GC ~= 0.43, well to the GC-rich side of the genome's mean of 0.393, so Gnocchi >= 4 is a cutoff of merely average stringency only in unusually GC-rich sequence; the arithmetic places 44% of all constrained-noncoding calls in the 7% of the noncoding genome that is most GC-rich. Refitting the regional adjustment on the scored population removes the dependence almost entirely: the retrained score's matched cutoff calls between 0.42% and 0.69% of windows throughout.

Correcting the bias redistributes discovery toward sequence where a call is worth more, and improves ranking where the score is actually used (Supporting Fig. 8). We evaluated both scores against the lax truth set of McHale et al., labelling each of 1,003,037 windows constrained if it overlaps a GeneHancer enhancer (30.9% positive), and read every quantity within GC bins. Because the enhancer base rate itself climbs 7.7-fold across those bins, we quantified the worth of a call by the positive likelihood ratio LR+ = P(call | constrained) / P(call | not constrained), which conditions on the true class on both sides and so is comparable between bins, rather than by lift or fold-enrichment, whose ceiling of 1/prevalence falls from 12.0 to 1.57 across the same range; the two measures disagree about the sign of the cross-bin trend on identical rows. Read at one fixed cutoff per score, each calling 10,051 windows, published Gnocchi's recall and LR+ run in opposite directions: recall climbs 45.7-fold with GC, from 0.33% to 15.07% of a bin's constrained windows, while LR+ falls monotonically from 2.06 to 1.25 (Supporting Fig. 8A). The score therefore spends its discovery budget almost entirely where a call carries least evidence, placing 2,618 of its calls in the bin where its LR+ is 1.25 and 38 in the bin where it is 2.06. The retrained score's recall is nearly flat over the same range, 2.03% to 0.93%, and at the same fixed budget it cuts the first figure to 154 and raises the second to 168 (Supporting Fig. 8B) -- debiasing does not call less, it calls elsewhere. The threshold each score would need in order to call a fixed 1% of every bin makes the same point in the score's own units and again without labels: published Gnocchi requires a cutoff climbing from 2.83 to 6.38, a 2.26-fold swing, where the retrained score requires something close to a constant, 3.01 to 3.16, a swing of 1.05-fold (Supporting Fig. 8F). Measured at those matched per-bin operating points, so that neither score is read at a different place on its own precision-recall curve, and at the top 1% of each bin -- the most constrained sequence either score can name -- the retrained score's LR+ exceeds published Gnocchi's in all five GC bins and significantly so in four: +37.7% [+1.7, +93.8], +6.3% [+0.8, +12.3], +8.5% [+2.3, +15.9], +33.0% [+8.8, +66.4] and +8.4% [-27.5, +58.6] from the lowest GC bin to the highest (Supporting Fig. 8I).

Averaged over all thresholds debiasing buys almost nothing, and discriminative power falls with GC content for reasons unrelated to the bias. Area under the precision-recall curve, normalised by each bin's positive-class fraction, falls from 1.518 to 1.199 across GC for published Gnocchi and from 1.554 to 1.298 for the retrained score, with pooled values of 1.321 and 1.341, and the one bin whose paired interval excludes zero favours PUBLISHED (-1.60% at GC 0.40-0.50) (Supporting Fig. 8C). Set beside the gains above, that is the central tension of this analysis rather than a null result: debiasing does not rank better on average, it ranks better at the top, and an average over all thresholds is where such a gain hides. It follows from how a GC-dependent bias acts -- within a bin five to ten GC points wide it is very nearly a common shift applied to positives and negatives alike, and a common shift cannot reorder a ranking. What the panel therefore isolates is the steep decline of discriminative power with GC, which survives a correction that removes essentially all of the bias, and which must accordingly reflect the observed-against-expected comparison carrying less constraint signal in GC-rich sequence relative to its own noise. Supporting Fig. 8A and 8B corroborate that decline in a prevalence-free unit, LR+ falling with GC for both scores, but both are read at a single operating point and only a threshold-free comparison can exclude its being confined to the top of the ranking.

A gain at the top with no net gain over all thresholds requires an offset elsewhere, and locating it needs the same comparison read at more than one depth. We therefore repeated the matched per-bin construction at calling rates of 50% and 99% of each bin as well as 1%, with the call at Gnocchi above the cutoff and a GeneHancer enhancer as the hit throughout, so that the three differ in operating point and in nothing else (Supporting Fig. 8D-8I). The cutoffs themselves move with GC at all three depths, which is the bias showing as a shift of the whole score within a bin rather than a stretch of one tail: published Gnocchi's median runs -0.96 to +2.32, a swing of 3.28 in z, where the retrained score's stays within 0.54 of itself, and its 1st and 99th percentiles swing 2.26-fold and 3.05 in z against 1.05-fold and 0.79 (Supporting Fig. 8D-8F). In the most GC-rich bin, half of published Gnocchi's windows therefore sit above z = 2.3, with Chen et al.'s cutoff only 1.7 further on. The retrained score leads in all five bins at the top 1% and trails in four of five at both the median (+0.1%, -1.3%, -3.1%, -3.0%, -3.0%) and the far end (-0.2%, -0.1%, -0.2%, -0.4%, -0.5%), so the gain is confined to the extreme top of the ranking and is paid for by a small, significant deficit through the rest of it (Supporting Fig. 8G-8I). Two things put that trade in proportion. In absolute terms the loss is slight where the gain is not: at the median neither score discriminates much, LR+ running 1.19 to 1.39 for both, so the largest deficit is a fall from 1.23 to 1.19 against a gain from 1.95 to 2.69 at the top of the same truth set. And the far-end comparison is pinned near 1.0 by arithmetic rather than by the scores agreeing, LR+ being the odds of precision over the odds of prevalence and a calling rate near 1 forcing the two together, so 8G is read for its sign and not its size. The deficit grows with GC at both depths, which locates whatever the retrained model discards in GC-rich sequence, where the regional adjustment varies most.

One caveat bounds all of this. Within a bin the GC bias is nearly a constant shift, which cannot reorder a ranking, so whatever separates the two scores at a matched rate is not the bias itself but within-bin variation in the regional adjustment -- information the published model carries and the retrained one discards along with the bias. Whether it is genuine signal about local mutation rate or a second artefact of the same fit is not resolved by these data, and we note that the thirteen candidate regional features include CpG island overlap and nucleosome occupancy, which correlate with regulatory annotation and so are not independent of a GeneHancer truth set.

We do not measure discovery in the left tail of the score, and an earlier version of this analysis did. A low Gnocchi score is not a claim that a window is unconstrained: the score is a two-sided z against a neutral expectation, so unconstrained sequence sits near zero, which is most of the genome, while the left tail is the opposite anomaly -- more variation than expected, whose leading explanations are hypermutability or misspecification of the mutation model rather than an absence of selection. Our own thresholds bear this out. Under the neutral null the 1st percentile of each GC bin would lie at z = -2.33; published Gnocchi's lies at -5.67, -6.04, -5.03, -4.14 and -2.98, so four of the five bins are far heavier in that tail than sampling noise would produce. Counting a non-enhancer as a correct call there would therefore test a hypothesis we have no truth set for, GeneHancer overlap being evidence about enhancer status and not about neutrality. Supporting Fig. 8G reads the same windows at the same cutoff in the direction the truth set does support, asking whether the windows above a left-tail cutoff are enhancers rather than whether those below it are not. A label-free test of what that tail contains is available in principle -- hypermutable windows should carry an elevated de novo mutation rate, which the same trio data used to fit the regional model can measure -- and we have not carried it out.

Three caveats bound these secondary analyses, none of which touches Fig. 5F, which uses no labels. The truth set is lax by construction, GeneHancer covering 18.4% of the noncoding genome against perhaps 4.51% under human-specific selection. Its positives are also GC-rich, the base rate climbing 7.7-fold across the bins drawn, from 8.3% to 63.9%, so a score that calls GC-rich sequence constrained performs well on it for that reason alone; published Gnocchi is such a score, and an unconditional comparison would therefore judge the correction by a statistic the bias inflates, which is why every quantity above is computed within a GC bin. Conditioning does not remove that skew -- positives remain enriched at the high-GC end of each bin -- but it changes what the skew implies: the truth set continues to favour published, so the retrained score's advantage is understated rather than flattered. We did not, however, equalise prevalence between bins by downsampling the positive class, as we did for the threshold-free comparison of Supporting Fig. 8C and as McHale et al. did for their Fig. 4: the quantities in Supporting Fig. 8A, 8B and 8D-8I are meant to report what an analyst obtains at the base rate actually present, LR+ conditions on the true class on both sides and so requires no such correction to be compared between bins, and pegging every bin to the positive fraction of the GC-poorest would discard about four fifths of the positives overall and some 95% of those in the most GC-rich bin, which is already the least powerful of the five. And in absolute positives recovered the redistribution costs, 5,485 against 4,396 at a common budget; that pooled comparison is the circular one described above, and a statement about the composition of the truth set rather than about either score.
