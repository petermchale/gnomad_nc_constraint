Gnocchi's cutoff does not mean the same thing across the genome, and retraining makes it do so. A z-score promises that a given value denotes the same departure from neutral expectation wherever it is measured, and that promise is what licenses attaching a single genome-wide cutoff to it. We tested it directly, using no truth set of any kind, by asking where a fixed cutoff falls within the score distribution of each GC bin (Fig. 5F). Chen et al.'s own cutoff of Gnocchi >= 4, at which they define constrained noncoding regions, sits at the 99.850th percentile of the most AT-rich windows drawn and the 58.3rd percentile of the most GC-rich, calling 0.150% of windows in the first and 41.7% in the last -- a 277-fold swing in how much sequence a single numeral selects. Genome-wide that same cutoff sits at the 99.34th percentile, and because the bins' calling rates average to that value and percentile is an affine function of calling rate, a score meeting its own promise would trace a horizontal line there and no other. Published Gnocchi crosses that line only at GC ~= 0.43, well to the GC-rich side of the genome's mean of 0.393, so Gnocchi >= 4 is a cutoff of merely average stringency only in unusually GC-rich sequence; the arithmetic places 44% of all constrained-noncoding calls in the 7% of the noncoding genome that is most GC-rich. Refitting the regional adjustment on the scored population removes the dependence almost entirely: the retrained score's matched cutoff calls between 0.42% and 0.69% of windows throughout.

Correcting the bias redistributes discovery toward sequence where a call is worth more, and improves ranking where the score is actually used (Supporting Fig. 8). We evaluated both scores against the lax truth set of McHale et al., labelling each of 1,003,037 windows constrained if it overlaps a GeneHancer enhancer (30.9% positive), and read every quantity within GC bins. Because the enhancer base rate itself climbs 7.7-fold across those bins, we quantified the worth of a call by the positive likelihood ratio LR+ = P(call | constrained) / P(call | not constrained), which conditions on the true class on both sides and so is comparable between bins, rather than by lift or fold-enrichment, whose ceiling of 1/prevalence falls from 12.0 to 1.57 across the same range; the two measures disagree about the sign of the cross-bin trend on identical rows. Read at one fixed cutoff per score, each calling 10,051 windows, published Gnocchi's recall and LR+ run in opposite directions: recall climbs 45.7-fold with GC, from 0.33% to 15.07% of a bin's constrained windows, while LR+ falls monotonically from 2.06 to 1.25 (Supporting Fig. 8A). The score therefore spends its discovery budget almost entirely where a call carries least evidence, placing 2,618 of its calls in the bin where its LR+ is 1.25 and 38 in the bin where it is 2.06. The retrained score's recall is nearly flat over the same range, 2.03% to 0.93%, and at the same fixed budget it cuts the first figure to 154 and raises the second to 168 (Supporting Fig. 8B) -- debiasing does not call less, it calls elsewhere. The threshold each score would need in order to call a fixed 1% of every bin makes the same point in the score's own units and again without labels: published Gnocchi requires a cutoff climbing from 2.83 to 6.38, a 2.26-fold swing, where the retrained score requires something close to a constant, 3.01 to 3.16, a swing of 1.05-fold (Supporting Fig. 8D). Measured at those matched per-bin operating points, so that neither score is read at a different place on its own precision-recall curve, and at the top 1% of each bin -- the most constrained sequence either score can name -- the retrained score's LR+ exceeds published Gnocchi's in all five GC bins and significantly so in four: +37.7% [+1.7, +93.8], +6.3% [+0.8, +12.3], +8.5% [+2.3, +15.9], +33.0% [+8.8, +66.4] and +8.4% [-27.5, +58.6] from the lowest GC bin to the highest (Supporting Fig. 8E).

Averaged over all thresholds, however, debiasing buys almost nothing -- and discriminative power falls with GC content for reasons unrelated to the bias. Scored over all thresholds the two scores are almost indistinguishable: area under the precision-recall curve, normalised by each bin's positive-class fraction, falls from 1.518 to 1.199 across GC for published Gnocchi and from 1.554 to 1.298 for the retrained score, with pooled values of 1.321 and 1.341, and the one bin whose paired interval excludes zero favours PUBLISHED (-1.60% at GC 0.40-0.50) (Supporting Fig. 8C). Set beside the gains above, that is the central tension of this analysis and not a null result: debiasing does not rank better on average, it ranks better at the top of the ranking, and an average over all thresholds is where such a gain can hide. This is not a null result about the bias but a consequence of how a GC-dependent bias acts: within a bin five to ten GC points wide it is very nearly a common shift applied to positives and negatives alike, and a common shift cannot reorder a ranking. What the panel therefore isolates is the steep decline of discriminative power with GC, which is left intact by a correction that removes essentially all of the GC bias, and which must accordingly reflect the observed-against-expected comparison carrying less constraint signal in GC-rich sequence relative to its own noise. Supporting Fig. 8A and 8B corroborate that decline in a prevalence-free unit, LR+ falling with GC for both scores; but both are read at a single operating point, and only the threshold-free comparison can exclude the possibility that the decline is confined to the top of the ranking. Where that gain is paid for is the question the last two panels ask, and it is not settled by the first five. An average over thresholds that shows no net gain, together with a gain at the top of the ranking, requires an offset somewhere; the natural candidate is the other extreme, since the two scores' precision-recall curves would then cross. We therefore repeated the matched per-bin construction at the BOTTOM 1% of each bin -- the windows each score is most confident are unconstrained -- with the call at z <= t and the hit a non-enhancer, a low Gnocchi being a claim that a window is unconstrained and the truth set's negatives being what such a claim is right about (Supporting Fig. 8F, 8G). The mirror is one keyword in the code and nothing else, so a difference between the two ends cannot be an artefact of measuring them differently. The direction reverses in every bin. Published Gnocchi's LR+ at the bottom 1% exceeds the retrained score's by 34.3%, 22.9%, 23.3%, 42.1% and 41.5% from the lowest GC bin to the highest, four of five intervals excluding zero, against a retrained lead in all five bins at the top. The two scores' precision-recall curves therefore cross, and the threshold-free wash of Supporting Fig. 8C is the average of a gain at one end of the ranking and a loss at the other. Two observations bound what this means. Both scores identify unconstrained sequence far more sharply than constrained -- published's LR+ runs 3.23 to 5.45 at the bottom against 1.32 to 1.95 at the top -- so the low tail is where this truth set carries most of its discriminating information, and it is the tail debiasing costs. And the gap cannot be the GC bias itself: within a bin that bias is nearly a constant shift, which cannot reorder a ranking, so what separates the two scores here is within-bin variation in the regional adjustment, information the published model carries and the retrained one discards along with the bias. Whether that information is genuine signal about local mutation rate or a second artefact of the same fit is not resolved by these data, and we do not claim it either way.

Three caveats bound these secondary analyses, none of which touches Fig. 5F, which uses no labels. The truth set is lax by construction, GeneHancer covering 18.4% of the noncoding genome against perhaps 4.51% under human-specific selection. Its positives are also GC-rich, the base rate climbing 7.7-fold across the bins drawn, from 8.3% to 63.9%, so a score that calls GC-rich sequence constrained performs well on it for that reason alone; published Gnocchi is such a score, and an unconditional comparison would therefore judge the correction by a statistic the bias inflates, which is why every quantity above is computed within a GC bin. Conditioning does not remove that skew -- positives remain enriched at the high-GC end of each bin -- but it changes what the skew implies: the truth set continues to favour published, so the retrained score's advantage is understated rather than flattered. We did not, however, equalise prevalence between bins by downsampling the positive class, as we did for the threshold-free comparison of Supporting Fig. 8C and as McHale et al. did for their Fig. 4: the quantities in Supporting Fig. 8A, 8B and 8D-8G are meant to report what an analyst obtains at the base rate actually present, LR+ conditions on the true class on both sides and so requires no such correction to be compared between bins, and pegging every bin to the positive fraction of the GC-poorest would discard about four fifths of the positives overall and some 95% of those in the most GC-rich bin, which is already the least powerful of the five. And in absolute positives recovered the redistribution costs, 5,485 against 4,396 at a common budget; that pooled comparison is the circular one described above, and a statement about the composition of the truth set rather than about either score.
