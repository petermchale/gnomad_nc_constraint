Figure 5 | Gnocchi's GC bias is introduced by its regional adjustment, and that adjustment is fit on a population of sites that is not the population it scores.

All panels are computed on the 693,270 putatively neutral noncoding 1 kb windows of Fig. 1, and GC content is the window's own. Gnocchi's expected count is built in two steps: step 1 predicts it from trinucleotide context and methylation level alone (E1), and step 2 multiplies each context's contribution by a regional adjustment r fit on de novo mutations (DNMs) and matched background sites (E2). R denotes a GC bin's aggregate adjustment, the ratio of summed expected counts Sum(E2)/Sum(E1). The bias of a rank curve is the mean of |mean rank - 0.5| over the GC bins drawn, so an unbiased metric has bias 0 and the number is the average distance of the plotted curve from the 0.5 line. That mean is unweighted over bins rather than over windows, so a sparse high-GC bin counts as much as a dense one; this is deliberate, the bias being largest in the tails, but it means the number is not a population average. Error bars are +/- 1 s.e.m. unless stated; vertical grey lines mark the mean GC of the population each panel draws. See Methods, "How Gnocchi's regional adjustment drives its GC bias", for every definition used here.

(A) The bias is introduced by step 2, not inherited from step 1. Mean standardised rank of each window's constraint score per GC bin, under step 1 alone and under the full published model, on the same 692,348 windows filtered and ranked jointly so that the only difference between the curves is whether r was applied. High rank means constrained, and an unbiased metric sits at 0.5 in every bin. The bias is 0.046 for the context-only model and 0.168 for Gnocchi as published, which reaches a mean rank of 0.92 in its highest GC bin: high-GC windows are systematically scored as constrained. Depletion rank, an independently constructed metric on its own overlapping windows, is overlaid for comparison (0.096); it is ranked within its own set, binned on its own GC edges, and drawn without error bars.

(B) The GC dependence of the applied adjustment is wholly non-CpG. R_eff, the solid orange curve, is what Gnocchi applies. It decomposes exactly, as an algebraic identity rather than a fit, into R_eff = Pi*R_CpG + (1 - Pi)*R_non, the same ratio of summed expected counts restricted to the four CpG contexts and to the other 28, with Pi the CpG share of step-1 expected counts. R_non rises from 0.96 to 1.45 across GC while R_CpG stays within 0.997-1.014. The dashed orange curve, drawn in R_eff's own colour and symbol because it is that same quantity with the non-CpG term switched off, is the counterfactual Pi*R_CpG + (1 - Pi) -- what Gnocchi would apply if it adjusted CpG contexts alone; it is flat within 0.4% while R_eff climbs to 1.33, so none of the applied trend survives removal of the non-CpG term. That flatness is a measurement, not an identity: Pi reaches 0.26 in the highest bin drawn (Supporting Fig. 7D), so a GC trend in R_CpG would appear here scaled by Pi rather than erased. R_CpG close to 1 is correct rather than a failure, because the large CpG methylation effect is already applied in step 1 (Supporting Fig. 7).

(C) The training set is not the scored population, and the territory that drops out is different rather than merely absent. Upper, the composition of the 4,367,225 non-CpG training sites per GC bin, each mapped to its containing 1 kb window and stacked by that window's stratum, so the four fractions sum to 1 in every bin. The scored share peaks at 0.36 near GC 0.35 and falls to 0.02 by GC 0.65, while the QC-fail share rises from about 0.15 in the GC bulk to 0.34. Lower, the log of each excluded stratum's empirical non-CpG DNM rate divided by the scored population's in the same bin, with error bars of +/- 1 delta-method binomial s.e.; zero means those sites are exchangeable with the scored ones as far as mutation rate is concerned. The coding stratum sits there (0.86-1.00x, flat across the range) and so does the QC-pass putatively nonneutral noncoding stratum (0.94-1.03x), so narrowing to the putatively neutral set costs sample size and nothing else. The QC-failing stratum runs 1.50-1.63x through the GC bulk and 3.39x by GC 0.58. The model is therefore fit on one population and applied to another, and the two come apart exactly where the bias in (A) is largest. That excess lies in sequence gnomAD could not call reliably, which is also where trio DNM calling is least reliable, so part of it is plausibly false-positive DNM calls rather than real mutation.

(D) Restricting the training set flattens the empirical DNM rate, and the fit can then track it. Upper, the empirical P(DNM | GC bin) of two training populations; lower, the fitted values over the same sites, on the same x axis and the same y range so that the two rows may be compared directly. A population keeps one symbol and one line style in both rows (squares and a solid line for the original training set, diamonds and a dashed line for the decontaminated one), so it reads as a single object down the figure; the empirical row additionally carries open symbols and error bars and the fitted row filled symbols and none, the standard error being a property of the measurement and not of a prediction. Each curve is divided by its own site-weighted mean across bins so that only shape is compared; the vertical distance between a population's two rows at fixed GC is a calibration gap conditioned on GC. On the training set as published (4,355,429 sites) the empirical rate rises 2.45x and then collapses above GC 0.66, and the fit, a smooth monotone surface chasing a curve that turns over, misses by 26% and 29% in opposite directions. Restricted to the scored population (930,417 sites) it rises smoothly by 1.60x with no turnover and the fit tracks it to within 6% through GC 0.58; the final bin, holding 670 sites, is the exception. A third training population, a size-matched random subsample drawn from the whole genome, lies on top of the original population's curves in both rows and is reported under (E) rather than drawn here. This panel diagnoses the fit: the level error it measures cancels in the ratio r, so (E) rather than (D) is the measurement of Gnocchi's bias.

(E) Refitting r on the scored population removes the bias. The statistic and the windows of (A), with a third score added, on the 692,325 windows surviving the joint filter across all three models. Exactly one thing changes from the published pipeline: training sites whose window lies outside the analysed set are dropped. Each curve's legend entry carries its bias. The retrained score falls below not only published Gnocchi but the context-only model itself, so a corrected r also repairs that model's droop at both GC extremes rather than merely undoing the damage. Two controls are reported numerically rather than plotted, each being indistinguishable from published Gnocchi: refitting through the same code on the full training set gives 0.168, so before and after differ by the intervention and not by whose code produced them, and refitting on a size-matched random subsample of the same training population (1,202,413 sites) gives 0.162, so the improvement is about which sites, not how many.


(F) The same repair, in the units the score is used in. For each GC bin of (E), and on (E)'s own windows and bins, the percentile at which a score's cutoff falls within that bin's OWN distribution of Gnocchi scores -- one cutoff per score, applied unchanged in every bin, so what moves between bins is the score's distribution and not the threshold. (E) and (F) are one measurement seen twice: (E)'s mean rank returns to 0.5 as (F)'s percentile flattens, and the vertical grey line marking mean GC sits at the same place in both. The cutoff is Chen et al.'s own, Gnocchi >= 4, at which they define constrained noncoding regions; the retrained score is given the threshold calling the same fraction of the whole population (z >= 3.237), because retraining shifts the entire z distribution and a common numeral would be a far stricter cutoff for one score than the other. A score that meant the same thing everywhere would draw a horizontal line, a fixed z sitting at a fixed percentile of every bin. Published Gnocchi does not: the numeral 4 is the 99.85th percentile of the most AT-rich windows drawn and the 58.333rd of the most GC-rich -- equivalently, it calls 0.150% of windows in the first and 41.667% in the last, a 277-fold swing, against the retrained score's 0.42% to 0.69% through the same range. The dashed horizontal line is the same quantity over a different population: where those cutoffs fall in the score distribution of the WHOLE window set, the 99.340th percentile genome-wide and a calling rate of 0.660%, against curves showing where each falls within its own GC bin. One line serves both scores because that number is what they were matched on. It is the panel's null in a strict sense -- the bins' calling rates average to it and percentile is affine in calling rate, so a curve flat across GC can only be flat ON this line -- and it is computed over every window, including bins too sparse to draw. What is pinned is that average, leaving any single bin free, so the retrained curve holds to the line through the GC bulk and departs in the sparse tails. The axis is logarithmic in the complementary calling rate and inverted so percentiles increase upward, a linear percentile axis compressing every AT-rich bin onto one line; ticks are set to round percentiles. A bin calling no windows has no percentile below 100 and is left undrawn. Bars are 95% Wilson intervals. No labels enter this panel, so unlike Supporting Figure 8 it rests on no truth set.

Fig. 5B shows R_CpG flat at about 1 across the whole GC range. This figure shows that this is correct rather than a failure of the model: the large, strongly GC-dependent CpG effect that a regional adjustment might have been expected to carry is already applied in step 1, and the contexts it applies to carry enough of the expected counts for its absence from step 2 to be informative. (A) The size of the methylation effect that step 1 absorbs. Dashed, the same observable read in a 1,000-genome downsample and normalised the same way, spanning 9.7-15.2x. Supporting Figure 8 | Debiasing leaves ranking over all thresholds almost unchanged because it trades one end of the ranking for the other: it improves discovery of the most constrained sequence, and costs discovery of the least, while replacing a cutoff that has to move across the genome with one that does not.

What Fig. 5F cannot say is whether the biased score was nevertheless the better detector, since bias and signal-to-noise act on discovery jointly and only one has changed. All seven panels answer that, on the lax truth set of McHale et al.'s Fig. 4A/B: a window is labelled constrained if it overlaps a GeneHancer enhancer, and the two Gnocchi variants of Fig. 5E take the place of their four metrics. The population is Fig. 5's own with one filter dropped -- enhancer-overlapping windows are kept, being the positive class -- so the retrained score is fit on the putatively neutral windows alone and evaluated on both halves. THE FIGURE READS IN THREE STEPS: (A) and (B) show where each score spends its calls, (C) gives the threshold-free verdict, and (D)-(G) go to the two ends of the ranking that verdict averages over -- (D) and (E) to the most constrained sequence each score can name, (F) and (G) to the least. TWO OPERATING POINTS run through the figure and must not be confused. (A) and (B) apply one fixed global cutoff per score -- published at Chen et al.'s Gnocchi >= 4, the retrained score at the value calling the same 1.00% of the population -- and let each bin's share fall where it may; that freedom is the bias. (D)-(G) instead match the calling rate WITHIN each bin at a flat 1%, which removes it, (D) and (F) drawing the thresholds that construction requires and (E) and (G) the gain measured at them, so each pair shares an x axis. Wherever a quantity is read ACROSS bins the figure plots LR+ = P(call | constrained) / P(call | not constrained) rather than lift: the enhancer base rate climbs 7.7-fold here, so lift, being precision over that rate, carries a ceiling of 1/r falling 12.0 to 1.57, and on these very rows lift falls 1.80 to 1.12 while the ceiling-free skill score rises 0.073 to 0.218 -- the choice of measure decides even the sign of the trend. LR+ conditions on the true class on both sides and the base rate cancels outright. Error bars: (C), (E) and (G) carry a 95% paired-bootstrap interval on the retrained score relative to published, each replicate resampling a bin's windows once and scoring both models on it, drawn on one curve because independent intervals would describe each level's uncertainty when the question is the gap; (A) and (B) carry Wilson intervals, their scores sitting at different operating points by construction; (D) and (F) carry none, a quantile of a million windows having no error worth drawing.

(A) Published Gnocchi calls most where a call is worth least. Recall (left axis, logarithmic) and LR+ (right axis, linear) against GC content, for published Gnocchi alone at Chen et al.'s cutoff of z >= 4, which calls 10,051 of the 1,003,036 windows drawn. The two curves run in OPPOSITE directions, and that opposition is the panel: recall climbs from 0.33% of a bin's constrained windows in the most AT-rich bin to 15.07% in the most GC-rich, a 45.7-fold swing, while LR+ falls monotonically from 2.06 to 1.25. The calls follow recall -- 38 windows called where LR+ is 2.06 against 2,618 where it is 1.25 -- so the score spends its discovery budget almost entirely where a call carries least evidence.

(B) The retrained score spreads the same budget evenly. Identical quantities and axis ranges to (A), for the score refit on the scored population, read at z >= 3.140 -- the cutoff calling the same 10,051 windows, so the budget is fixed and only its distribution free. Recall is nearly flat, 2.03% to 0.93% against published's 45.7-fold climb, and the calls move accordingly: 168 in the most AT-rich bin against 38, and 154 in the most GC-rich against 2,618. Debiasing does not spend less, it spends elsewhere, so 'debiasing costs recall' is a statement about GC-rich sequence only. LR+ still declines, 3.11 to 1.50; that residual is the signal-to-noise fall of (C) and no part of the bias, and a reader who takes this panel as 'still not fixed' has read (C)'s quantity off these axes. The comparison between the two scores is (E), not the gap between (A) and (B), whose curves sit at different operating points in every bin.

(C) Scored over all thresholds, the two are almost indistinguishable, and that is a finding rather than a null result. Area under the precision-recall curve within each GC bin, normalised by that bin's positive-class fraction so 1.0 is random guessing, after downsampling the positive class to give every bin the same prevalence. A GC-dependent bias is very nearly a common shift applied to every window in a narrow bin, positives and negatives alike, and a common shift cannot change a ranking; so auPRC within a GC bin is close to blind to the bias by construction. Two things follow, and the rest of the figure exists to separate them. First, performance falls steeply with GC for both scores -- published 1.518 to 1.199 across the bins drawn, the retrained score 1.554 to 1.298 -- and that decline survives debiasing intact; since the bias is the one thing that changed, what remains must be signal-to-noise, which McHale et al. conjectured and this measures. Second, debiasing buys almost nothing on this measure: pooled over all bins the two are 1.321 and 1.341, and the one bin whose interval excludes zero is NEGATIVE, -1.60% at GC 0.40-0.50. Those pooled values are not drawn, having no place on axes conditional on GC everywhere. Error bars are the 95% paired-bootstrap interval of the retrained score relative to published, carried on that curve alone. But auPRC integrates over the WHOLE recall axis, so a wash here is consistent with a gain at one end of the ranking and a loss at another. That is what (D)-(G) find: the retrained score leads in all five bins at the top 1% and trails in all five at the bottom, so this panel's verdict is an average over two crossing precision-recall curves rather than a statement that nothing changed.

(D) The threshold each score would need in order to be applied evenly, which is the bias in the score's own units and uses no labels at all. For each GC bin, the Gnocchi value calling the top 1% of that bin. Published Gnocchi answers with a curve, climbing from 2.83 in the most AT-rich bin drawn to 6.38 in the most GC-rich, a swing of 2.26-fold; the retrained score with something close to a constant, 3.01 to 3.16, a swing of 1.05-fold. Like Fig. 5F, and unlike the panels that need the truth set, this rests on the score and GC content alone. It is the inverse view of Fig. 5F rather than a repetition: 5F fixes the threshold and reads the calling rate, this fixes the rate and reads the threshold, each natural for a different reader -- 5F for someone holding a cutoff, (D) for someone holding a discovery budget. It is also (E)'s x-axis made visible, since (E)'s gains are measured at exactly these thresholds.

(E) At the top of the ranking, the retrained score is the better detector in every GC bin. The ratio of the retrained score's LR+ to published Gnocchi's, per GC bin, with the calling rate matched within each bin so both call 1% of every bin and neither is read at a different point on its own precision-recall curve. That matching is necessary because Fig. 5F is precisely the statement that the two call very different fractions of each bin. A ratio is plotted rather than two levels because level curves invite a reading of each curve against itself across GC, which is (A) and (B)'s job, whereas the only question here is whether retraining helps within a bin; the dashed line at 1.0 is no gain. The ratio is an ODDS ratio: within a bin the base rate cancels from either measure, but a fold increase in precision is bounded by one over published Gnocchi's own precision, a ceiling falling about 14-fold across these bins, whereas odds(precision) = LR+ x odds(base rate) makes the LR+ ratio exactly the odds ratio and so comparable bin to bin. The gains are +37.7% [+1.7, +93.8], +6.3% [+0.8, +12.3], +8.5% [+2.3, +15.9], +33.0% [+8.8, +66.4] and +8.4% [-27.5, +58.6] from the lowest GC bin to the highest, with four of five intervals clear of 1.0; quote the middle bins, since the most AT-rich gain is largest but rests on 219 called windows per score. Each score is cut at its own 99th percentile WITHIN each bin, so published Gnocchi's threshold varies bin to bin -- panel (D) is that variation drawn -- and equals 4 nowhere in particular; it is far above 4 in the most GC-rich bin, where an unmatched cutoff of 4 calls 13.97% of windows rather than 1%, and below 4 in the most AT-rich, where it calls 0.17%. This panel therefore describes a score nobody applies, which is what isolates ranking from threshold placement; what the score does as used is (A), (B) and Fig. 5F. Set against (C), it localises the gain: debiasing does not rank better on average, it ranks better HERE.

Recall is not drawn beside (E), though it is drawn in (A) and (B). With the calling rate matched within a bin, Bayes gives lift = precision / r = recall / k, and with k the same 1% in every bin and for both scores, lift, precision and recall are one quantity in three units, so a recall curve beside (E) would carry no information of its own. In (A) and (B) the cutoff is global instead, k varies 45.7-fold across GC, and the identity stops being a rescaling. The translation is still worth making, since recall is the unit an analyst acts in: read as the per-bin precision ratio, the retrained score's calls are correct 1.30 times as often as published Gnocchi's in the most AT-rich bin drawn and 1.02 times as often in the most GC-rich. Those are precision ratios and therefore smaller than the odds ratios (E) plots; quoting one for the other is the easiest error to make with this panel.

(F) The same construction at the other end of the score. For each GC bin, the Gnocchi value calling the BOTTOM 1% of that bin -- the windows each score is most confident are unconstrained. Like (D) it uses no labels, and like (D) it is the x-axis of the panel beneath it. Read the two threshold panels together: if published Gnocchi's cutoff has to climb with GC at BOTH ends of the distribution, its bias is a shift of the whole score within a bin rather than a distortion of one tail, which is what licenses reading (E) and (G) as two views of one ranking. It does, though not monotonically: published Gnocchi's bottom-1% cutoff runs -5.67, -6.04, -5.03, -4.14, -2.98 from the most AT-rich bin drawn to the most GC-rich, rising by 3.05 in z over its last four bins, while the retrained score's stays within 0.79 of itself (-4.85, -5.64, -5.56, -5.55, -5.20). Its top-1% cutoff climbs too, 2.83 to 6.38 in (D), so the published score's whole distribution slides with GC rather than one of its tails stretching.

(G) Whether the gain at the top is paid for at the bottom. The ratio of the retrained score's LR+ to published Gnocchi's for calls at the BOTTOM 1% of each bin, constructed exactly as (E) but with the call at z <= t and the hit a NON-enhancer, since a low Gnocchi is a claim that a window is unconstrained and the truth set's negatives are what such a claim is right about. The mirror is one keyword in the code and nothing else, so precision, LR+, the Wilson bounds and the paired bootstrap are the same functions of a relabelled problem, and a difference between (E) and (G) cannot be an artefact of measuring the two ends differently. NOTE THAT THIS IS NOT LR-: the likelihood ratio of a negative TEST, P(no call | constrained) / P(no call | not constrained), sits within 1% of 1.0 in every bin at a 1% calling rate -- failing the cutoff is 99% of windows and so carries almost no information, and its published-to-retrained ratios span 0.9939 to 0.9993 against 1.06 to 1.38 for LR+ -- whereas the likelihood ratio of the rare LOW-tail event, which is what (G) reports, has room to move. It is paid for. The ratio falls BELOW 1.0 in all five GC bins -- -34.3% [-67.4, +0.0], -22.9% [-28.8, -15.1], -23.3% [-28.4, -18.2], -42.1% [-51.1, -31.1] and -41.5% [-56.9, -22.0] from the lowest GC bin to the highest, four of five intervals clear of 1.0 -- exactly reversing (E), where the retrained score leads in all five. The two scores' precision-recall curves therefore cross, and (C)'s wash is the average of a gain at one end and a loss at the other, which is what that panel could not resolve. Read the levels as well as the ratio: published's LR+ here is 3.23 to 5.45, well above the 1.32 to 1.95 it manages in (E), so on this truth set both scores identify unconstrained sequence far more sharply than constrained, and published does it better. Note what this cannot be. Within a bin the GC bias is nearly a constant shift, and a constant shift cannot reorder a ranking, so the gap here is not the bias itself but within-bin variation in the regional adjustment -- information the published model carries and the retrained one gives up along with the bias. Whether that information is real signal about mutation rate or a second artefact is not settled by this figure.

Three caveats belong with this figure and not with Fig. 5. First, the truth set is lax by construction: GeneHancer covers 18.4% of the noncoding genome while perhaps 4.51% is under human-specific selection, so enhancer overlap is a weak proxy and 30.9% of the windows here are positive. Second, and more sharply, its positives are GC-rich: the base rate climbs 7.7-fold across the bins drawn, from 8.3% to 63.9%, so a score that calls GC-rich sequence constrained scores well on it for that reason alone. Published Gnocchi is that score, so an unconditional comparison is circular: it judges the correction by a statistic the bias inflates. That is also why the redistribution of (A) and (B) costs in absolute positives found -- 5,485 against 4,396 at a common budget -- which is a statement about the truth set rather than about either score. Third, every panel here is therefore computed WITHIN a GC bin; and since positives stay enriched at the high-GC end of every bin, the truth set favours published even after conditioning, so the retrained score's advantage in (E) is understated rather than flattered. None of the three touches Fig. 5F, which uses no labels. A note on how that varying base rate is handled, since one obvious remedy is deliberately not applied to (A), (B) and (D)-(G): prevalence could be equalised between bins by downsampling the positive class, which is what (C) does and what McHale et al. do for their Fig. 4, and which would make lift comparable across bins by fixing its 1/r ceiling. It is not done here for three reasons. First, it would answer a different question. (C) is a threshold-free ranking statistic whose LEVEL must be comparable between bins for one random-classifier baseline at 1.0 to be valid everywhere, so downsampling is exactly right there; (A), (B) and (D)-(G) instead report what an analyst obtains on applying a cutoff, and precision in GC-rich sequence is high because enhancers are genuinely dense there -- a balanced precision is a number no one will ever encounter. Second, the same comparability is available without discarding anything: recall, the false-positive rate and LR+ = TPR/FPR are class-conditional, so the prevalence cancels analytically and on every window in hand, whereas downsampling reaches the same end by making the prevalence constant, which is to say by throwing data away. Third, the cost of doing so would fall exactly where these panels are weakest: the peg is set by the GC-poorest bin, so the most GC-rich bin would retain about 5% of its positives and its calls at a 1% rate would fall from 188 to roughly 74, and that is already the one bin of five whose interval in (E) spans no difference.
