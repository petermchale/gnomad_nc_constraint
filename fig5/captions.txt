Figure 5 | Gnocchi's GC bias is introduced by its regional adjustment, and that adjustment is fit on a population of sites that is not the population it scores.

All panels are computed on the 693,270 putatively neutral noncoding 1 kb windows of Fig. 1, and GC content is the window's own. Gnocchi's expected count is built in two steps: step 1 predicts it from trinucleotide context and methylation level alone (E1), and step 2 multiplies each context's contribution by a regional adjustment r fit on de novo mutations (DNMs) and matched background sites (E2). R denotes a GC bin's aggregate adjustment, the ratio of summed expected counts Sum(E2)/Sum(E1). The bias of a rank curve is the mean of |mean rank - 0.5| over the GC bins drawn, so an unbiased metric has bias 0 and the number is the average distance of the plotted curve from the 0.5 line. That mean is unweighted over bins rather than over windows, so a sparse high-GC bin counts as much as a dense one; this is deliberate, the bias being largest in the tails, but it means the number is not a population average. Error bars are +/- 1 s.e.m. unless stated; vertical grey lines mark the mean GC of the population each panel draws. See Methods, "How Gnocchi's regional adjustment drives its GC bias", for every definition used here.

(A) The bias is introduced by step 2, not inherited from step 1. Mean standardised rank of each window's constraint score per GC bin, under step 1 alone and under the full published model, on the same 692,348 windows filtered and ranked jointly so that the only difference between the curves is whether r was applied. High rank means constrained, and an unbiased metric sits at 0.5 in every bin. The bias is 0.046 for the context-only model and 0.168 for Gnocchi as published, which reaches a mean rank of 0.92 in its highest GC bin: high-GC windows are systematically scored as constrained. Depletion rank, an independently constructed metric on its own overlapping windows, is overlaid for comparison (0.096); it is ranked within its own set, binned on its own GC edges, and drawn without error bars.

(B) The GC dependence of the applied adjustment is wholly non-CpG. R_eff, the solid orange curve, is what Gnocchi applies. It decomposes exactly, as an algebraic identity rather than a fit, into R_eff = Pi*R_CpG + (1 - Pi)*R_non, the same ratio of summed expected counts restricted to the four CpG contexts and to the other 28, with Pi the CpG share of step-1 expected counts. R_non rises from 0.96 to 1.45 across GC while R_CpG stays within 0.997-1.014. The dashed orange curve, drawn in R_eff's own colour and symbol because it is that same quantity with the non-CpG term switched off, is the counterfactual Pi*R_CpG + (1 - Pi) -- what Gnocchi would apply if it adjusted CpG contexts alone; it is flat within 0.4% while R_eff climbs to 1.33, so none of the applied trend survives removal of the non-CpG term. That flatness is a measurement, not an identity: Pi reaches 0.26 in the highest bin drawn (Supporting Fig. 7D), so a GC trend in R_CpG would appear here scaled by Pi rather than erased. R_CpG close to 1 is correct rather than a failure, because the large CpG methylation effect is already applied in step 1 (Supporting Fig. 7).

(C) The training set is not the scored population, and the territory that drops out is different rather than merely absent. Upper, the composition of the 4,367,225 non-CpG training sites per GC bin, each mapped to its containing 1 kb window and stacked by that window's stratum, so the four fractions sum to 1 in every bin. The scored share peaks at 0.36 near GC 0.35 and falls to 0.02 by GC 0.65, while the QC-fail share rises from about 0.15 in the GC bulk to 0.34. Lower, the log of each excluded stratum's empirical non-CpG DNM rate divided by the scored population's in the same bin, with error bars of +/- 1 delta-method binomial s.e.; zero means those sites are exchangeable with the scored ones as far as mutation rate is concerned. The coding stratum sits there (0.86-1.00x, flat across the range) and so does the QC-pass putatively nonneutral noncoding stratum (0.94-1.03x), so narrowing to the putatively neutral set costs sample size and nothing else. The QC-failing stratum runs 1.50-1.63x through the GC bulk and 3.39x by GC 0.58. The model is therefore fit on one population and applied to another, and the two come apart exactly where the bias in (A) is largest. That excess lies in sequence gnomAD could not call reliably, which is also where trio DNM calling is least reliable, so part of it is plausibly false-positive DNM calls rather than real mutation.

(D) Restricting the training set flattens the empirical DNM rate, and the fit can then track it. Upper, the empirical P(DNM | GC bin) of two training populations; lower, the fitted values over the same sites, on the same x axis and the same y range so that the two rows may be compared directly. A population keeps one symbol and one line style in both rows (squares and a solid line for the original training set, diamonds and a dashed line for the decontaminated one), so it reads as a single object down the figure; the empirical row additionally carries open symbols and error bars and the fitted row filled symbols and none, the standard error being a property of the measurement and not of a prediction. Each curve is divided by its own site-weighted mean across bins so that only shape is compared; the vertical distance between a population's two rows at fixed GC is a calibration gap conditioned on GC. On the training set as published (4,355,429 sites) the empirical rate rises 2.45x and then collapses above GC 0.66, and the fit, a smooth monotone surface chasing a curve that turns over, misses by 26% and 29% in opposite directions. Restricted to the scored population (930,417 sites) it rises smoothly by 1.60x with no turnover and the fit tracks it to within 6% through GC 0.58; the final bin, holding 670 sites, is the exception. A third training population, a size-matched random subsample drawn from the whole genome, lies on top of the original population's curves in both rows and is reported under (E) rather than drawn here. This panel diagnoses the fit: the level error it measures cancels in the ratio r, so (E) rather than (D) is the measurement of Gnocchi's bias.

(E) Refitting r on the scored population removes the bias. The statistic and the windows of (A), with a third score added, on the 692,325 windows surviving the joint filter across all three models. Exactly one thing changes from the published pipeline: training sites whose window lies outside the analysed set are dropped. Each curve's legend entry carries its bias. The retrained score falls below not only published Gnocchi but the context-only model itself, so a corrected r also repairs that model's droop at both GC extremes rather than merely undoing the damage. Two controls are reported numerically rather than plotted, each being indistinguishable from published Gnocchi: refitting through the same code on the full training set gives 0.168, so before and after differ by the intervention and not by whose code produced them, and refitting on a size-matched random subsample of the same training population (1,202,413 sites) gives 0.162, so the improvement is about which sites, not how many.


(F) The same repair, in the units the score is used in. For each GC bin of (E), the percentile at which a score's cutoff falls within that bin's OWN distribution of Gnocchi scores -- one cutoff per score, the value given in the legend, applied unchanged in every bin, so that what moves from bin to bin is the score's distribution and not the threshold -- computed on (E)'s own windows and bins, so that the two panels are one measurement seen twice: (E)'s mean rank returning to 0.5 and (F)'s percentile flattening; the vertical grey line marking mean GC is at the same place in both for that reason. The cutoff is Chen et al.'s own, Gnocchi >= 4, at which they define constrained non-coding regions; the retrained score is instead given the threshold that calls the same fraction of the whole population (z >= [F-THRESH]), because retraining shifts the entire z distribution and a common numeral would be a far stricter cutoff for one score than for the other. The swing across GC is computed within a single score and is unaffected by that choice. A score that meant the same thing everywhere would draw a horizontal line here, since a fixed z would sit at a fixed percentile of every bin. The dashed horizontal line is which horizontal line, and it is the same quantity as the curves over a different population: it marks where those very cutoffs fall in the score distribution of the WHOLE window set -- the [F-MATCHED-PCTL]th percentile genome-wide, equivalently a calling rate of [F-MATCHED]% of all windows -- against curves showing where each falls within its GC bin's own distribution. One line serves both scores because that number is exactly what they were matched on: Gnocchi >= 4 calls [F-MATCHED]% of the population, and the retrained score's cutoff is the value of its own z calling the same fraction. It is computed over every window, including those in bins too sparse to draw, so it is the operating point the scores are used at and not an average of the points plotted. It is the panel's null in a strict sense rather than a loose one: the bins' calling rates average to that value exactly, and percentile is an affine function of calling rate, so a curve that is flat across GC can only be flat ON this line -- a score meeting its own promise would not merely draw some horizontal line but this one. What is pinned is that average, which leaves any single bin free, so the retrained curve holds to the line through the GC bulk and departs in the sparse tails. The published curve meets it well to the GC-rich side of the mean-GC mark, and the offset between the two intersections is itself a statement -- a calling rate climbing near-exponentially with GC has a window-weighted mean far above its value at a typical window, so Gnocchi >= 4 is a cutoff of merely average stringency only in sequence appreciably more GC-rich than average. Published Gnocchi does not: the numeral 4 is the [F-PCTL-LO]th percentile of the most AT-rich windows drawn and the [F-PCTL-HI]th percentile of the most GC-rich, a stringent cutoff in one part of the genome and a permissive one in another. Equivalently, and this is the same curve read from the other end, it calls [F-LO]% of windows in the first and [F-HI]% in the last, a [F-FOLD]-fold swing; the retrained score calls [F-FLAT]% throughout. The axis is logarithmic in that complementary calling rate and inverted so that percentiles increase upward: a linear percentile axis would compress every AT-rich bin onto one line at the top and lose the panel's content, and the tick values are set to round percentiles for the same reason. A bin in which a score calls no windows at all has no percentile below 100 and is left undrawn rather than plotted at the edge. Bars are 95% Wilson intervals. No labels of any kind enter this panel: where a cutoff falls in the local score distribution is a property of the score and of GC content, so unlike Supporting Figure 8 it rests on no truth set.

Supporting Figure 7 | Why the CpG contexts correctly receive no GC-dependent regional adjustment.

Fig. 5B shows R_CpG flat at about 1 across the whole GC range. This figure shows that this is correct rather than a failure of the model: the large, strongly GC-dependent CpG effect that a regional adjustment might have been expected to carry is already applied in step 1, and the contexts it applies to carry enough of the expected counts for its absence from step 2 to be informative. See Methods, "How Gnocchi's regional adjustment drives its GC bias".

(A) The size of the methylation effect that step 1 absorbs. Solid, the fitted C>T probability for each of the four CpG contexts against methylation level, each divided by its own value at level 0: it spans 3.0-4.3x from level 0 to level 15, within a single trinucleotide context and the largest single rate effect in the model. Dashed, the same observable read in a 1,000-genome downsample and normalised the same way, spanning 9.7-15.2x. The gap between them is saturation of the polymorphism probability at the larger cohort size, which cancels against the observed counts and is not an error in the model.

(B) High-GC CpGs are CpG islands. Fraction of CpG-context training sites that are hypomethylated (methylation level <= 1; blue, left axis) and mean methylation level (grey, right axis), per GC bin. The hypomethylated fraction runs 2.5% through the GC bulk and reaches 0.92 in the top bin, while mean methylation falls from about 6.4 to 0.47 over the same range.

(C) So their DNM rate collapses. Empirical P(DNM) at CpG-context training sites per GC bin: flat at 0.532 through the GC bulk and falling to 0.283 in the top bin, a 1.9-fold fall that tracks (B). Error bars +/- 1 s.e.m. These are case-control-sampled training sites, so the y axis is a probability under the training distribution and not a genome-wide mutation rate.

(D) And these contexts are not a rounding error. Pi, the CpG share of a GC bin's step-1 expected counts and the weight that appears in Fig. 5B's decomposition, rises from 0.038 to 0.264 across the range. Had R_CpG carried a GC trend, it would have reached the applied multiplier scaled by up to 0.26, so the flatness of Fig. 5B's counterfactual is a measurement and not an artefact of negligible weight.

Supporting Figure 8 | Correcting the bias leaves ranking almost unchanged when it is scored over all thresholds, and improves it at the threshold the score is used at.

What Fig. 5F cannot say is whether the biased score was nevertheless the better detector, since bias and signal-to-noise act on discovery jointly and only one of them has changed. Both panels answer that, on the lax truth set of McHale et al.'s Fig. 4A/B: a window is labelled constrained if it overlaps a GeneHancer enhancer, and the two Gnocchi variants of Fig. 5E take the place of their four constraint metrics. The population is Fig. 5's own with a single filter dropped -- the enhancer-overlapping windows are kept rather than excluded, because they are the positive class -- so the retrained score is fit on the putatively neutral windows alone and evaluated here on both halves. In both panels the retrained curve carries its 95% confidence interval relative to published, from a paired bootstrap in which each replicate resamples a GC bin's windows once and scores both models on that same resample. The interval is drawn on one curve and not two deliberately: independent intervals would describe the uncertainty of each level when the question is the gap between them, and would be far wider, because the two scores are columns of one table and the variability in which windows the truth set happens to contain cancels in the difference. A bar clear of the published marker is a real difference.

(A) Scored over all thresholds, the two are almost indistinguishable -- and that is the finding, not a null result. Area under the precision-recall curve within each GC bin, normalised by that bin's positive-class fraction so that 1.0 is random guessing, after downsampling the positive class to give every bin the same prevalence. A GC-dependent bias is very nearly a common shift applied to every window in a narrow bin, positives and negatives alike, and a common shift cannot change a ranking; so auPRC within a GC bin is close to blind to the bias by construction. The consequence is the panel's real content: performance falls steeply with GC for both scores -- published 1.518 to 1.199 across the bins drawn -- and that decline survives debiasing intact. Since the bias is the one thing that changed, what remains must be signal-to-noise, which McHale et al. conjectured and this measures. The eye is drawn to the gap in the top bin (1.199 against 1.298 on 6,217 windows); its interval is what shows that this is the one difference that does not survive.

(B) At the operating point the score is used at, the retrained score ranks better in every GC bin. Lift -- precision divided by the bin's own base rate, so 1.0 is random guessing -- with the calling rate matched within each bin rather than genome-wide, so that both scores call 1.002% of every bin and neither is read at a different point on its own precision-recall curve. That matching is necessary because Fig. 5F is precisely the statement that the two call very different fractions of each bin, and lift falls as a threshold loosens. Read the thresholds carefully, because they are not a single number: Gnocchi >= 4 enters only by fixing the matched rate, as the fraction of the whole population published Gnocchi calls at that cutoff (1.002%); each score is then cut at its own 98.998th percentile WITHIN each bin, so published Gnocchi's own threshold varies from bin to bin and equals 4 nowhere in particular. It is far above 4 in the most GC-rich bin, where the unmatched cutoff of 4 calls 13.97% of windows rather than 1%, and below 4 in the most AT-rich, where it calls 0.17%. This panel therefore describes a score nobody applies; that is deliberate, and it is what isolates ranking from threshold placement. What the score does as it is actually used is Fig. 5F. Lift is higher for the retrained score in all five bins and significantly so in four: +33.3% [+4.1, +80.0], +4.0% [+0.4, +7.8], +4.2% [+1.3, +7.7], +10.8% [+3.2, +20.3] and +2.2% [-8.6, +13.6] from the lowest GC bin to the highest. The middle bins are the ones to quote; the +33.3% clears zero but rests on 220 called windows per score. This does not contradict (A): auPRC integrates over the whole recall axis, lift at a 1% calling rate probes only the top of the ranking, and the two scores' precision-recall curves cross, the retrained one higher at low recall and converging by mid-recall. Both are correct, and they weight the recall axis differently -- which is a reason to prefer (B) for a score nobody applies at 60% recall.

Recall is not drawn as a third panel, because with the calling rate matched within a bin it is this panel in other units: Bayes gives lift = precision / r = recall / k with r the bin's base rate and k the calling rate, so recall = lift x k with k the same 1.002% in every bin and for both scores, and a recall curve would carry the same five per-bin gains. The translation is worth making even though the panel is not, since recall is the unit an analyst acts in: read as recall, the lifts above say that the retrained score catches 2.42% of a bin's constrained windows against published Gnocchi's 1.81% in the most AT-rich bin drawn, then 1.59 against 1.53, 1.34 against 1.29, 1.25 against 1.13 and 1.15 against 1.13% in the most GC-rich. The identity is verified numerically in the accompanying notebook rather than assumed. Note what those figures are not: they are not the claim that debiasing raises recall in absolute terms, which is a question about Fig. 5F's unmatched thresholds, where recall is redistributed across GC rather than gained -- 0.33 to 2.03% in the most AT-rich bin and 15.07 to 0.93% in the most GC-rich, at a fixed genome-wide budget of 10,051 called windows for each score.

Three caveats belong with this figure and not with Fig. 5. First, the truth set is lax by construction: GeneHancer covers 18.4% of the noncoding genome while perhaps 4.51% of it is under human-specific selection, so enhancer overlap is a weak proxy for constraint and 30.9% of the windows here are positive. Second, and more sharply, at a matched calling budget GC content alone reaches a lift of 2.15 on this truth set against published Gnocchi's 1.77, so an unconditional comparison on enhancer overlap is substantially a GC-content contest and cannot by itself adjudicate between two constraint scores. Neither caveat touches Fig. 5F, which uses no labels.
